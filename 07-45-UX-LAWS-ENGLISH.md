# 45 UX Laws, Effects & Design Heuristics — English Study Reference

_Last curated: 2026-10-25._

> **Provenance:** User-provided Hindi/English fragments from an Indian Instagram series introduced as “45 UX Laws.” **The Instagram handle and the complete original 45-episode list could not be independently verified.** Days 1–11 below are translations/reconstructions of the supplied fragment, not full video transcripts. The **45-entry reference below is an editorially curated learning list**, not a claim that these are the creator's exact Day 1–45 order.
>
> **Terminology note:** Psychology effects, quantitative laws, cognitive concepts, Gestalt principles, interface techniques and usability heuristics are not all scientific “laws.” The title preserves the source theme; each entry is classified more carefully. These are *design considerations*, **not** mandates to install features or to override the project's [design anti-patterns](01-DESIGN-UX.md).

## 2. Complete curated reference — 45 distinct principles

**Selection method:** Entries 01–30 are from Jon Yablonski’s [Laws of UX index](https://lawsofux.com/), which mixes “laws,” principles, effects and concepts. Entries 31–45 extend that index with well-established HCI laws, Gestalt principles and Nielsen Norman Group heuristics. This completes a **45-item study set**, **not a reconstructed, verified 45-video playlist**.

### A. The 30-item Laws of UX reference

| # | Principle | Kind | Explanation | Practical check |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 01 | **Aesthetic–Usability Effect** | Effect | Attractive interfaces can seem easier to use, even when they are not. | Test task success, not just visual appeal. |
| 02 | **Choice Overload** | Effect | Too many options can overwhelm choice. | Group choices; don't assume exactly three cards is necessary. |
| 03 | **Chunking** | Technique | Meaningful groups make information easier to process. | Split a complex form into logical field groups. |
| 04 | **Cognitive Bias** | Umbrella concept | Predictable judgment tendencies alter perception and decisions. | Avoid exploiting biases with fake urgency or social proof. |
| 05 | **Cognitive Load** | Concept | Mental work needed to understand and use an interface. | Remove avoidable steps, jargon and simultaneous demands. |
| 06 | **Doherty Threshold** | HCI heuristic | Fast responses sustain user flow; the often-quoted ~400 ms is a guideline, not a universal SLA. | Give immediate honest feedback during longer work. |
| 07 | **Fitts’s Law** | Motor-performance law | Reaching a target depends on target size and distance. | Make frequent taps reachable and hit areas comfortable. |
| 08 | **Flow** | Psychological state | Focused engagement can arise when challenge matches skill. | Reduce unnecessary interruptions during sustained tasks. |
| 09 | **Goal-Gradient Effect** | Behavioral effect | Motivation often rises as a goal comes closer. | Show accurate progress where a real goal exists. |
| 10 | **Hick’s Law** | Choice-time law | Decision time increases with number and complexity of choices. | Organize navigation and expose relevant options first. |
| 11 | **Jakob’s Law** | UX heuristic | People expect familiar interaction conventions from other products. | Keep navigation and basic controls recognizable. |
| 12 | **Law of Common Region** | Gestalt law | Elements within the same bounded area tend to be seen as grouped. | Use a container only when a group needs a boundary. |
| 13 | **Law of Proximity** | Gestalt law | Nearby elements tend to seem related. | Place each form label near its control. |
| 14 | **Law of Prägnanz** | Gestalt law | People favor simple coherent interpretations of ambiguous forms. | Avoid overloaded visual shapes and confusing silhouettes. |
| 15 | **Law of Similarity** | Gestalt law | Objects that look alike tend to be grouped. | Keep equivalent controls consistently styled. |
| 16 | **Law of Uniform Connectedness** | Gestalt law | Visually connected items appear related. | Use lines or connectors where they communicate a real relationship. |
| 17 | **Mental Model** | Cognitive concept | Users have expectations about how a product operates. | Match conceptual structure to user expectations; validate with testing. |
| 18 | **Miller’s Law** | Memory-related heuristic | A classic finding reports roughly seven plus or minus two chunks in immediate memory; not a fixed menu limit. | Favor meaningful groups and recognition over arbitrary item limits. |
| 19 | **Occam’s Razor** | Reasoning heuristic | Prefer fewer unsupported assumptions when alternatives work equally well. | Choose the simplest design that meets real requirements. |
| 20 | **Paradox of the Active User** | HCI observation | Users often start interacting instead of reading instructions. | Teach actions contextually; don't force long tours. |
| 21 | **Pareto Principle** | Empirical heuristic | A minority of inputs often accounts for much of the outcome. | Prioritize frequent high-impact user tasks without ignoring others. |
| 22 | **Parkinson’s Law** | Work-time observation | Work tends to expand to available time. | Keep task steps focused; it is not a screen-layout mandate. |
| 23 | **Peak-End Rule** | Memory effect | Experience judgments often emphasize the peak and ending. | Make success, failure recovery and exit moments clear and humane. |
| 24 | **Postel’s Law** | Robustness principle | Historically: tolerate valid input variation while emitting clear output. | Accept reasonable formatting while still enforcing strict security validation. |
| 25 | **Selective Attention** | Cognitive concept | People prioritize information relevant to their goals. | Don't compete for attention with decorative banners. |
| 26 | **Serial Position Effect** | Memory effect | First and last items often have recall advantages. | Put essential navigation where users can find it; verify usability. |
| 27 | **Tesler’s Law** | Design heuristic | A system has essential complexity that must be handled somewhere. | Let the system handle complexity rather than pushing it onto users. |
| 28 | **Von Restorff Effect** | Isolation effect | An item distinct from its peers is more memorable. | Reserve contrast for truly important actions or warnings. |
| 29 | **Working Memory** | Cognitive system | A limited capacity keeps and manipulates information briefly. | Retain user context between screens. |
| 30 | **Zeigarnik Effect** | Memory effect | Interrupted or unfinished tasks can remain mentally salient. | Support saved drafts and honest resume flows; avoid manipulating anxiety. |

*Source for entries 01–30: [Laws of UX](https://lawsofux.com/). Descriptions and checks are newly authored summaries.*

### B. Fifteen supplementary principles (not verified as the creator's Days 12–45)

| # | Principle | Kind | Explanation | Practical check | Reference |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 31 | **Weber’s Law** | Psychophysical law | Detectability of a stimulus change often depends on its proportional, not absolute, magnitude. | Check whether size/contrast changes are noticeable at relevant baselines. | [Psychophysics/HCI](https://www.textbookofusability.com/chapter/towards-a-science-of-design.html) |
| 32 | **Steering Law** | Motor-performance law | Moving a pointer through a constrained path gets harder as the path becomes longer or narrower. | Avoid narrow hover corridors and fragile nested menus. | [HCI paper](https://www.textbookofusability.com/references/accotzhai1997.html) |
| 33 | **Power Law of Practice** | Learning regularity | Repeated practice can reduce task time, often with diminishing gains. | Support shortcuts for experienced users without harming beginners. | [HCI textbook](https://academic.oup.com/book/60808/chapter/528999264) |
| 34 | **Law of Closure** | Gestalt principle | People perceptually fill gaps to see incomplete shapes as wholes. | Use recognizable incomplete shapes only when interpretation remains clear. | [Gestalt (IxDF)](https://ixdf.org/literature/topics/gestalt-principles) |
| 35 | **Law of Continuity** | Gestalt principle | The eye tends to follow smooth paths and continuous alignment. | Keep related steps aligned and reading flows coherent. | [Gestalt (IxDF)](https://ixdf.org/literature/topics/gestalt-principles) |
| 36 | **Figure–Ground Principle** | Gestalt principle | People separate foreground subjects from backgrounds. | Give controls readable contrast and distinct focus. | [Gestalt (IxDF)](https://ixdf.org/literature/topics/gestalt-principles) |
| 37 | **Law of Symmetry and Order** | Gestalt principle | Symmetrical arrangements tend to read as grouped and orderly. | Use balance when it clarifies relationships, not as a rigid style rule. | [Gestalt (IxDF)](https://ixdf.org/literature/topics/gestalt-principles) |
| 38 | **Law of Common Fate** | Gestalt principle | Elements moving or pointing together are perceived as related. | Coordinate meaningful grouped movement; avoid gratuitous animation. | [Gestalt (IxDF)](https://ixdf.org/literature/topics/gestalt-principles) |
| 39 | **Recognition Rather than Recall** | Usability heuristic | Visible or retrievable choices reduce memory burden. | Show needed context, labels and selected settings. | [NN/g heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/) |
| 40 | **Progressive Disclosure** | UI design technique | Defer less frequently needed complexity until requested. | Expose advanced settings on demand while keeping essential options available. | [NN/g progressive disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/) |
| 41 | **Visibility of System Status** | Usability heuristic | Users should know what the application is doing and the outcome of actions. | Show accurate loading, saved, error and progress states. | [NN/g heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/) |
| 42 | **Error Prevention** | Usability heuristic | Remove avoidable traps and prevent costly mistakes before they occur. | Validate early and protect destructive actions. | [NN/g heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/) |
| 43 | **User Control and Freedom** | Usability heuristic | People need clear exits, cancellation and undo where possible. | Offer undo, back and cancel when consequences warrant. | [NN/g heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/) |
| 44 | **Consistency and Standards** | Usability heuristic | Equivalent actions, language and patterns should behave consistently. | Maintain consistent controls across pages and platform conventions. | [NN/g heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/) |
| 45 | **Match Between System and Real World** | Usability heuristic | Use language, ordering and concepts users recognize. | Write labels for users, not internal engineering jargon. | [NN/g heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/) |

## 3. Applying the list without violating the user's design rules

- **Choose based on an observed interface problem.** Hick's Law does not mean every design should use three cards, and the Von Restorff Effect does not license meaningless colored badges.
- **Beautiful appearance is not proof of usability.** Use real usability tests, error handling and accessibility tests.
- **The “seven plus or minus two” memory finding is not a hard navigation count.** Modern research distinguishes memory for items versus meaningful chunks; favor reducing memory burden.
- **Feedback and motion must communicate state.** Loading indicators, actual progress, confirmation and useful hover/focus states are distinct from decorative animations.
- **Respect agency.** Goal-Gradient and Zeigarnik ideas must not create fake progress, pressure, anxiety, or manipulative retention loops.
- **Avoid duplicating content as fake certainty.** Day 5 is not identified, the Instagram source is unverified, and the supplementary principles are not presented as missing original episodes.
